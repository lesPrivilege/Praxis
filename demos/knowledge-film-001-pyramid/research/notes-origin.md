## 1. Birth year and place; work before business school

**1a. Birth year 1934 (January 1934).** Verdict: **only secondary, weak.**
Source: Wikidata (WD), "date of birth: January 1934", references are a 1940 census index (ancestry.com) and a yearbook index (myheritage.com); neither was opened by me. Locator: Statements > date of birth. Type S. Do not put on screen.

**1b. Birthplace.** Verdict: **not found.** No primary or reliable secondary source states it. (The Wikidata census reference points to an Ohio listing, which is a hint only. Wikidata lists residence London and Cleveland, not birth.)

**1c. Before business school: "secretary at a railroad company in Cleveland".** Verdict: **conflicting in detail; the Cleveland part is not found for this job.**
- T1 (S): she "was a secretary at an American railway company in the 1950s 'making 400 bucks a month'"; her boss was in his 70s and she feared he would die; Harvard "allowed applications from people without an undergraduate degree", so she "took the exam and got in". Locator: paragraph beginning "She recounted how, long before Harvard". The employer is not named and Cleveland is not mentioned.
- K1 (S, agent bio): "Barbara began her career on the staff of Cyrus Eaton, the industrialist who founded the famous Pugwash Conferences"; worked on the team that organised the conferences; "In 1961 she left Mr Eaton to attend the Harvard Business School". Locator: first paragraph under her name.
- SU (S) says the same (Eaton, Pugwash, left in 1961).
- Possible reconciliation (my inference, not a sourced fact): Wikipedia's Cyrus S. Eaton article, citing the Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, says Eaton became board chairman of the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway in 1954, was based in Cleveland, and was born in 1883 (so in his 70s from 1953). That fits "railway company" plus "boss in his 70s". No source says Minto worked for the C&O, or that "secretary" and "Eaton's staff" are the same job.
- M1 and B-pages say nothing on pre-HBS work.
Verdict: **only secondary; do not state employer or city.** Safe wording if needed: "she began as a secretary" (T1, her own account as reported by Telegraph).

## 2. Harvard Business School

**2a. Graduated 1963.** Verdict: **confirmed by primary source.** M1: "In 1963, Barbara graduated from Harvard Business School, which had only just begun granting MBA degrees to women." Locator: section "The Firm years", first sentence. Also B2 /course: "Barbara Minto holds an MBA from Harvard Business School."

**2b. Entry year 1961.** Verdict: **only secondary.** K1 ("In 1961 she left Mr Eaton to attend the Harvard Business School"), SU, Wikidata (educated at HBS 1961 to 1963, citing the HBS/Telegraph page, which does not actually say 1961). The two-year programme makes 1961 arithmetically plausible but no primary source states it.

**2c. "One of the first eight women admitted to the MBA programme, class of 1963".** Verdict: **conflicting; the claim as phrased is wrong. Do not use it.**
- T1 (S): "in 1963 was one of eight ladies to be among the first to graduate with an MBA in a class of 600 – and the only one resident in the UK". Locator: second paragraph. This is a reporter paraphrasing a speech; the number of women graduating in 1963 is not confirmed elsewhere. Wikipedia repeats "one of only eight women to graduate in a class of 600".
- K1 (S): "One of the eight pioneering women permitted to attend the Harvard Business School in 1963" (this wording is the "admitted in 1963" version).
- HBS's own history (H1, H2, P): "the first eight female students enrolled in the two-year MBA programme" in September 1963 and joined the class that graduated in **1965**; the faculty vote was December 1962. Before that, women could take the one-year Harvard-Radcliffe Program in Business Administration (HRPBA) and from 1959 apply for the second year of the MBA; H2/C1 say three women received Harvard MBAs in 1960 and the number reached 11 in 1965 (C1, S).
- So Minto (graduated 1963) belongs to the earlier HRPBA-route cohort, not the "first eight" of 1963. The K1 wording seems to confuse the two. How a secretary without a degree reached the second-year route is not explained in any source I found.
- Telegraph, Goodreads and a blog also call her "first female graduate of HBS (1963)". That is contradicted by H2/C1 (women graduated in 1960). Do not use.
Authority: HBS's own pages outrank the Telegraph and the agent copy for school history; M1's phrase "only just begun granting MBA degrees to women" is consistent with HBS.

**2d. Her own words on the class.** Verdict: confirmed (P). M1, "The Firm years": classmates "could be really unpleasant" and said "You're taking a space a man could have!" Also in M1 near the end: "The school would very likely not have let me in had I applied these days." (T1 adds a classmate's view that the men were too busy and ambitious to treat the women differently; S.)

## 3. McKinsey

**3a. Hired 1963, Cleveland, then London.** Verdict: **confirmed by primary source.** M1: "Barbara was with the Firm from 1963 to 1973, first in Cleveland, then in London." and "The Firm's recruiters let it be known they wanted a female MBA for the new office being opened in Cleveland. Barbara applied for the job and got it". Locator: paragraph after "Among her vivid memories"; section "The Firm years".

**3b. Wording of the "first woman" claim.** Verdict: **confirmed wording; variants are weaker.**
- McKinsey's own words (P, M1, opening): "The first female MBA professional hire our Firm ever made". Use this exact phrasing and attribute to McKinsey.
- Variants: Wikipedia "first female MBA hired by McKinsey"; Telegraph "their first female consultant in the USA" (S, reporter paraphrase; adds a country qualifier McKinsey does not use); a supply-chain blog says "first female consultant in the USA" (S).
- M1 also says "although she didn't immediately thrive as a number-crunching consultant, she had writing skills and soon started building on them", and quotes John Tomb (Cleveland office manager, 1986 oral history, as relayed by McKinsey) that she took over editing reports and "eventually I guess every office in the Firm used her on a part-time basis".

## 4. Move to London and the discovery

**4a. Moved to London in 1966.** Verdict: **confirmed by primary source.** M1: "In 1966, Barbara moved to London."

**4b. Assignment: "helping European consultants write reports in English".** Verdict: **not confirmed as phrased; the core idea is confirmed.**
- M1 does not give a formal London assignment. It says that on trips to the Paris and Düsseldorf offices she "encountered the same writing problems she had seen in the U.S. and U.K." (McKinsey's paraphrase), and that she was already editing reports. Nothing about "in English" or about non-native speakers as the brief.
- SU (S): "When she moved to Europe in 1966 to help the European teams improve their writing, she saw that the challenges to communicating clearly were not an American issue but a human one." Supports "help European teams with writing", still not "in English".
- Not found in any primary source: any statement that the London brief was English-language editing.

**4c. Her own words on the discovery.** Verdict: **confirmed by primary source (P, M1).** Exact quotes, all from the section "The Firm years":
- "The problem was the thinking, not the language," she says. (8 words)
- Same paragraph: "People were starting to write without working out their thinking in advance." (12 words)
- Then: "But how does one go about figuring out one's thinking in advance?" (11 words) — her question that led to the library reading (see item 8).
Her earlier observation (M1, above the "Look familiar?" caption): editing reports, "Barbara had noticed she always seemed to be reorganizing ideas into a pyramid shape." This sentence is McKinsey's narration, not a direct Minto quote.
First teaching material (M1): her "first manual, 'Skillful Writing through Structured Thinking'", which colleagues in every office adopted; she then turned the idea into a course taught at Firm offices around the world. B2 /course confirms: her course "is based on a similar course she developed and taught for several years at McKinsey & Company".

## 7. MECE

**7a. What she says.** Verdict: **confirmed by primary source (M1).** Locator: paragraph after the "Look familiar?" caption.
- Definition (McKinsey's phrasing): "The concept required the groups of ideas to be MECE—divided pieces that were mutually exclusive of each other and collectively exhaustive in terms of the whole."
- Pronunciation (McKinsey's phrasing): "We usually say it 'Mee-cee', but Barbara pronounces it with one syllable, meece, rhyming with 'niece' or 'Greece.'"
- Exact short quote: "I invented it, so I get to say it how to pronounce it," she says. (The page itself has "say it how to pronounce it"; the page title reads "say how to pronounce it". Quote the body text if you show the line, or the title as a title.)
- Immediate amendment: "Well, it wasn't really I, actually, it was Aristotle," she says, "but I was the first one to abbreviate it and apply it to analyzing groups of ideas." (27 words if quoted in full; trim to the Aristotle clause or paraphrase.)
- Her three rules as quoted: "The point above has to be a summary of those below, because it is derived from them." and "You can't derive an idea from a grouping unless the ideas in the grouping are logically the same, and in logical order."

**7b. Evidence against her being the originator.** Verdict: **only secondary; she herself concedes Aristotle.**
- Her own concession (above) is primary. She claims only the abbreviation and its application to idea groups.
- W1 (Wikipedia, "Historical Antecedents"): S. R. Ranganathan's 1937 "Prolegomena to Library Classification" states canons of exhaustiveness and exclusiveness. I did not open the book; Wikipedia pointer only.
- Wikipedia's "developed in the late 1960s" is not in M1. Do not state a year for coining MECE.
- Safe framing: she coined the acronym and applied it; she credits the logic to Aristotle.

## 8. Intellectual sources she credits

Verdict: **confirmed by primary source for the McKinsey-page names; Miller confirmed only via the book's table of contents (catalogue transcription) and secondary summaries; Herbert Simon not found.**

And her quote: "And I had the great good fortune to be in the London office with colleagues whose brains had been trained at Oxford and Cambridge, and who also had questions on structure."

- Search-result summaries named other thinkers (Wittgenstein, Chomsky, Popper, Kuhn); I could not trace them to a page. **Unverified; do not use.**
- Book's own claim as quoted by SU: "The mind automatically sorts information into distinctive pyramidal groupings in order to comprehend it." (S quoting the book; not checked against the book.)

## Safe to state in the film (confirmed by a primary source)

1. She graduated from Harvard Business School in 1963 (M1; B2 /course). She "only just" caught the period when HBS had begun granting MBAs to women (M1's words).
2. McKinsey hired her in 1963 for the new Cleveland office; she was with the Firm from 1963 to 1973, first in Cleveland, then in London (M1).
3. McKinsey describes her as "the first female MBA professional hire our Firm ever made" (M1). Attribute the wording to McKinsey.
4. She moved to London in 1966 (M1).
5. On trips to Paris and Düsseldorf she met the same writing problems; her conclusion in her words: "The problem was the thinking, not the language." (M1)
6. She turned the idea into a course and a first manual, "Skillful Writing through Structured Thinking" (M1).
7. After the 1973 oil crisis cut the London office, she was among those who left and set up her own business teaching the method (M1).
8. Her book: "The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking" (earlier edition) and "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving" (1996, her authoritative edition, 12 chapters and 3 appendices); Pearson/Pitman sold the older edition (B2 /textbook; M1).
9. MECE: she says she coined it and pronounces it "meece"; she then credits Aristotle and claims only the abbreviation and its use for analysing groups of ideas (M1).
10. She read Piaget, "Levi Strauss" (as spelled on McKinsey's page), the Bourbaki mathematicians, Talcott Parsons, Mortimer Adler and Jacob Bronowski while looking for books on the structure of thought (M1).
11. Her site describes the method (organise ideas as a pyramid under a single point; guide the reader down the pyramid) and says the book has been translated into several languages including Simplified and Traditional Chinese ("Simple and Mandarin Chinese" in her wording) (B1, B2 /concept).
12. Her course: 2 days, 10-14 participants, 45-minute individual tutoring, based on a McKinsey course she taught for years (B2 /course).
13. The McKinsey page says she has taught "tens of thousands" of people and, in the 80s and 90s, "as many as four courses a month" (M1; her figures, as reported by McKinsey).
14. HBS history (P): the first eight women admitted directly to the full two-year MBA enrolled in September 1963 and graduated in 1965 (H1, H2).
