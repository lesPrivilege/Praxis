## How to read the verdicts

- **confirmed by primary**: stated on Minto's own site, in an interview with Minto, or in a library catalogue record.

## A. What the method says

### 1. The three rules

- **Claim.** Minto states three rules. In the book's order:
  1. "Ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them."
  2. "Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea."
  3. "Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered."
- **Her shortcut for rule 2.** "Be sure you can label the ideas with a plural noun." Her examples are recommendations, reasons, problems, changes to be made.
- **Minto's own later paraphrase.**
  - "I saw it meant there were only three logical rules to obey."
  - "The point above has to be a summary of those below, because it is derived from them."
  - You cannot derive an idea from a grouping unless the ideas are "logically the same, and in logical order".
- **Verdict.**
  - The existence of three rules: confirmed by primary (the McKinsey alumni interview with Minto).
  - The exact book wording: secondary only (page-cited quotation, Minto [1996] 2018, p. 9 and p. 10).
- **Sources and locators.**
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch1_why_pyramid.html, heading "Pyramid rules".
  - https://www.mckinsey.com/alumni/news-and-events/global-news/alumni-news/barbara-minto-mece-i-invented-it-so-i-get-to-say-how-to-pronounce-it, section "The Minto Pyramid Principle" (near the phrase "only three logical rules to obey").
  - The 1996 table of contents puts the rules in Ch. 1 "Why a Pyramid Structure?" (see item 10).
- **Note on order.** Some summaries list "same kind" first (for example StrategyU, https://strategyu.co/pyramid-principle-partone/). Follow the book order above.
- **Note on wording.** The interview paraphrase does not use the word "rules" for the second and third rules separately. If a title card quotes the rules, use the book wording and flag it as "Minto 1996, ch. 1".

### 2. The kinds of logical order

- **Claim.**
  - The book lists four ways to order ideas in a grouping, in Ch. 1 (p. 10-11): deductively (major premise, minor premise, conclusion); chronologically; structurally ("Boston, New York, Washington"); comparatively ("first most important, second most important").
  - Later, in the Part 2 chapter "Imposing Logical Order", she reduces the orders for inductive groupings to **three: time order, structural order, degree order**. Each is tied to a source of the grouping: causes of an effect give time order; dividing a whole gives structural order; classifying like things gives degree order (p. 76).
  - Deductive groupings take the order of the argument itself.
- **Verdict.** Your recollection is confirmed, with a refinement. "Comparatively" in Ch. 1 becomes "degree order" (the Pearson edition's table of contents says "ranking order"). Secondary only for the wording (page-cited quotes). The chapter titles are primary-grade (see item 10).
- **Sources and locators.**
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch1_why_pyramid.html, heading "4 possible ways of logically ordering ideas" (p. 10-11).
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch6_logical_order.html, first table "There are 3 possible logical orders" (p. 76) and the quote "Do you find an order (time, structure, degree)?" (p. 93).
  - Library of Congress record of the Pearson edition, Ch. 7 headings "Time order / Structural order / Ranking order": https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/toc/fy0906/2009396730.html

### 3. Vertical and horizontal relationships

- **Claim.**
  - The book's Ch. 2, "The Substructures Within the Pyramid", has three sub-sections: "The Vertical Relationship", "The Horizontal Relationship", "The Introductory Flow".
  - Vertical is a question/answer dialogue with the reader. In the top-down procedure Minto writes "Now you begin you question/answer dialouge with the reader" (typo as transcribed on the notes site; p. 22-23).
  - Horizontal is a logical argument that is either **deductive or inductive**. Cross-checked in an unlicensed excerpt, re-verify in a lawful copy: she says the points must present "a clear inductive or deductive argument, one or the other, but not both at once", and that these are the only two types in a grouping.
  - Deductive means three steps: a statement about a situation, a comment on it, then a conclusion. Inductive means ideas that can be labelled with one plural noun (her "tanks at the Polish border" example).
- **Verdict.** Terms confirmed. Secondary only for wording (page-cited note quote for the dialogue; table-of-contents headings are primary-grade).
- **Sources and locators.**
  - https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/toc/fy0906/2009396730.html, Ch. 2 headings.
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch3_build_a_pyramid.html, quote in step 5 (p. 22-23).
  - Adrian S. Tam's reading notes, which assign the same terms to Ch. 2: https://www.adrian.idv.hk/2017-12-20-minto/ (secondary; his chapter numbers follow another edition).

### 4. The introduction: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer, and the acronym

- **Claim.**
  - The book builds the introduction as a story: Situation, then Complication, then Question, with the Answer as the top box of the pyramid. Page-cited: "Begin your thinking with the Situation" (p. 31). The top-down procedure has you "Decide the Question", "Write down the Answer", "Identify the Situation", "Develop the Complication" (p. 22-23).
  - **The acronym.** Minto's own site brands it the **"SCQ Framework"** (marked ℠/™ on different pages). It describes it as the "Situation, Complication, Question Framework" for "identifying the question in your reader's mind". I found no page of her own site that uses "SCQA".
  - Third parties routinely expand SCQ to "Situation, Complication, Question, Answer" (for example 12manage, Entrepreneur). SCQA is therefore the secondary-literature form. SCQ is her own label.
  - Whether the book's text itself abbreviates the sequence (S-C-Q-A or SCQ): **not found** in anything I could verify.
- **Verdict.** Terms Situation, Complication, Question: confirmed by primary (her site). Answer as the last element in the sequence: secondary only. The acronym "SCQA" as hers: **not found**. Your recollection is half right: her acronym is SCQ.
- **Sources and locators.**
  - https://www.barbaraminto.com/ and https://www.barbaraminto.com/course (footer "SCQ Framework"; home-page text "Situation, Complication, Question Framework (SCQ Framework™)").
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch3_build_a_pyramid.html, steps 1-6 (p. 22-23) and "Begin your thinking with the Situation" (p. 31).
  - https://www.12manage.com/methods_minto_pyramid_principle.html, "(Situation, Complication, Question, Answer)" under the SCQ heading (secondary).
- **Optional.** Her p. 37 table (manager138 ch. 4) gives four reader questions: What should we do? How do we do it? Should we do it? Why did it happen? Page-cited, secondary.

### 5. Deduction vs induction at the key line level

- **Claim.** Confirmed that she advises induction at the key line. Her stated reasons, page-cited:
  - Deduction at that level makes the reader wait: "you force him to reenact your entire problem-solving process before he receives his reward" (p. 64).
  - "At the paragraph level, deductive arguments are lovely ... but inductive reasoning is always easier to absorb at higher levels" (p. 67).
  - Rule of thumb on order: present the action before the argument (p. 66).
  - Deductive chains: no more than four points, no more than two "therefore" points (p. 68).
- **Your recollection that she says the reader must "hold premises in mind":** **not verified as Minto's wording.** The sentence about holding multiple statements in the head is the notes-site author's gloss, not a quoted line from the book. Do not attribute it to her.
- **Verdict.** Induction at the key line: secondary only (page-cited quotes). The "hold premises" reason: not found as her reasoning.
- **Source and locator.** https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch5_deduction_and_induction.html, headings "Why not to use deduction for key point" and "Present action, then argument". Bookey's chapter summary also lists "favor induction over deduction" at key-line level: https://www.bookey.app/book/the-minto-pyramid-principle (weak, AI-style summary).

### 7. Top-down vs bottom-up construction

- **Claim.** Two procedures, Ch. 3 "How to Build a Pyramid Structure".
  - **Top-down** (preferred, p. 22-23), six steps: draw a box for the Subject; decide the Question; write the Answer if known; identify the Situation; develop the Complication; recheck Question and Answer.
  - **Bottom-up** (p. 26): "1. List all the points you think you want to make. 2. Work out the relationships between them. 3. Draw conclusions."
  - Minto's site puts the thinking itself bottom-up: people "grouping together low-level facts", "drawing an insight", "forming a new grouping".
  - Page-cited summary line: the clearest documents present "from the top down ... even though the original thinking will have been done from the bottom up" (p. 7).
- **Verdict.** Confirmed by primary for the idea (her site); secondary only for the step lists (page-cited).
- **Sources and locators.**
  - https://www.barbaraminto.com/concept, bulleted list under "Concept".
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch3_build_a_pyramid.html, headings "Top Down (preferred)" and "Bottom Up".
  - https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch1_why_pyramid.html, "Summary" (p. 7).

### 8. How many items per grouping; Miller; "rule of three"

- **Claim.**
  - She cites **George A. Miller**, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two". The 1996 table of contents has the section "The Magical Number Seven" (p. 3).
  - What she says (cross-checked in an unlicensed excerpt and quoted by the Phlix and Deepstash summaries): the mind cannot hold more than about seven items in short-term memory; some hold nine, some five; "A convenient number is three, but of course the easiest number is one." When items rise above about four or five, the mind starts to group them.
  - **So your recollection needs correcting in one respect.** Minto does mention three, as a "convenient" number, and calls one easiest. She does not make "the rule of three" a rule. Her stated limit is the seven-ish ceiling, and the working advice is to group once a list passes four or five.
  - Other limits in her text: deductive chains have no more than four points and no more than two "therefore" points (p. 68).
  - Origin of the "rule of three" in consulting: **not found**. A Think Insights explainer says consultants "use the rule of 3" without a source (https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/pyramid-principle). I found no source tying the three-point habit to Minto beyond her "convenient number" line.
- **Verdict.** Miller citation and "convenient number is three": secondary only (quoted by summaries; read in an unlicensed excerpt). "Rule of three is not her rule": confirmed in the sense that no rule was found; "she says nothing about three" is false.
- **Sources and locators.**
  - https://www.sebastienphlix.com/book-summaries/minto-pyramid-principle (search result excerpt: "Focus on three ideas at most, only one is even better" with the quote; the first clause is the summariser's, not Minto's).
  - https://deepstash.com/community/article/187644/the-pyramid-principle (section "The Magical Number Seven").
  - Library of Congress record for the section title: https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/toc/fy0906/2009396730.html (Pearson edition, p. 7).
- **Warning.** The Phlix summary adds "at most" to a statement she words as "convenient". Do not use "at most three" as a quotation of Minto.

### 9. MECE; logic trees

- **Claim: definition and place in the method.**
  - Minto's definition, page-cited: when you divide a whole into parts, the pieces must be "Mutually exclusive of each other" and "Collectively exhaustive in terms of the whole". She adds, "I abbreviate this mouthful to MECE" (p. 82, in the discussion of structural order, Part 2).
  - So MECE belongs to **structural order**: dividing a whole into its parts. It is not a separate fourth rule.
  - Minto on the term's origin: she says it "wasn't really I, actually, it was Aristotle", but she was "the first one to abbreviate it and apply it to analyzing groups of ideas" (McKinsey alumni interview).
  - The notes site gives an inconsistent reference for p. 82. Treat the page as unconfirmed.
- **Claim: logic trees and issue analysis.**
  - **Logic trees are in the book.** The 1996 Ch. 9 has "Developing Logic Trees" (p. 156), within "Structuring the Analysis of the Problem". The Pearson edition's Ch. 8 has "Five typical logic trees" and "Use of the logic tree concept".
  - The 1996 Ch. 9 also has "Performing an Issue Analysis" with sub-sections "The History" (p. 163) and "The Misconceptions" (p. 166). I could not read the text of those pages.
  - The notes site lists the trees she describes: task trees, "Activity Structure" (negative task) trees, decision trees. Page-cited, secondary.
  - Whether the term "issue tree" appears in her book: **not found**. Whether the issue tree is a separate McKinsey tool: **not found** (no McKinsey source located).
- **Verdict.** MECE definition and place: secondary only (page-cited quote; interview is primary on her authorship claim). Logic tree in her book: confirmed by primary-grade table of contents (Library of Congress; 1996 table of contents seen in the book's own front matter, see item 10).
- **Sources and locators.**

  - https://www.mckinsey.com/alumni/news-and-events/global-news/alumni-news/barbara-minto-mece-i-invented-it-so-i-get-to-say-how-to-pronounce-it ("it was Aristotle").

## B. Misreadings, misuse, limits

### 11. Answer-first and hypothesis-driven work: confirmation bias

- **Finding.** I did **not** find a named academic or well-known author who attacks Minto's method itself on confirmation-bias grounds. What I found:
  - **Sedulo Group**, "The Case for Evidence-First Problem Solving" (2025-08-20): says the hypothesis-based problem-solving model behind "answer-first" consulting leads consultants to "unconsciously seek data that supports their initial hunch" and ends in "an elegant, well-structured solution to the wrong problem". Corporate blog, no named author, and it attacks hypothesis-based problem solving, not Minto by name. https://sedulogroup.com/blog-post/evidence-first-problem-solving/ (sections "The Problem with Hypothesis-Based Problem Solving"). Weak as authority.
  - **Gary Klein**, Psychology Today, 2019-05-05, "The Curious Case of Confirmation Bias": argues that the confirmation-bias concept itself rests on an over-read Wason (1960) study. Useful as a counterweight, so the film does not overstate the bias claim. https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/seeing-what-others-dont/201905/the-curious-case-of-confirmation-bias
  - **Rittel and Webber (1973)** on wicked problems: "the problem can't be defined until the solution has been found" (see item 12). That attacks premature framing, not Minto by name. The link to answer-first is your inference.
- **What Minto herself separates.** The book's Appendix A, "Problem solving in structureless situations", distinguishes "Analytical Abduction" and "Scientific Abduction" and has "Generating Hypotheses" and "Devising Experiments" sub-sections (table of contents only; text not read).
  - Minto: "The pyramid is a tool to help you find out what you think" (McKinsey alumni interview). It is a thinking aid. She does not describe it as picking evidence for a fixed answer.
  - "answer-first" in the Minto sense is a reader-facing order of presentation (answer at the top of the document). The hypothesis-driven sense (decide an answer before the analysis) is a consulting practice that grew alongside it. Keep the two apart on screen.
- **Verdict.** Named critique tying Minto to confirmation bias: **not found**. The critique of hypothesis-driven consulting: **secondary only**, weak.

### 12. MECE and complex, interdependent problems

- **Claim.**
  - **Roger Martin** (Rotman; *Playing to Win* blog, 2024-11-25), "Generating Strategy Possibilities: Mutually Exclusive & Collectively Exhaustive vs. Happy Stories".
    - Credits Minto with creating MECE "for the purpose of helping to structure an argument", and has "no quarrel with it in that domain".
    - But he is "not a fan of MECE in possibilities generation". Says it is "too technocratic" and that the exhaustiveness requirement "seems extreme". Partly overlapping options can hold real synergies.
    - On how it spread: "I don't know how it happened, or whether Minto advocated that or not."
    - This is a **named, citable** critique of MECE being stretched past its original use, and it is also a statement about misuse. It is not a systems-thinking critique.
    - https://rogermartin.medium.com/generating-strategy-possibilities-b1c4aeae877a
  - **Rittel and Webber, "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning", *Policy Sciences* 4 (1973): 155-169.** Anchor for "wicked" problems, and not about MECE. Verbatim lines from the text:
    - "There is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem" (property 1).
    - "Wicked problems have no stopping rule" (property 2).
    - Property 1 argues that to describe a wicked problem in sufficient detail "one has to develop an exhaustive inventory of all conceivable solutions ahead of time". That is a point about exhaustiveness, but R&W do not mention MECE or Minto. The link to MECE is **your inference** and must be framed so on screen.
    - Also: "Social problems are never solved. At best they are only re-solved."
    - Open PDFs: http://wendynorris.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Rittel-and-Webber-1973-Dilemmas-in-a-general-theory-of-planning.pdf and https://urbanpolicy.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Rittel-Webber_1973_DilemmasInAGeneralTheoryOfPlanning.pdf. SEBoK summary: https://sebokwiki.org/wiki/Dilemmas_in_a_General_Theory_of_Planning
  - **Systems-thinking critiques of MECE by named authors:** **not found.** The "real world is messy, interdependencies exist" statements I met are in practitioner or case-prep pages with no named author (for example https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/strategy-frameworks/mece-principle/). Do not present them as scholarship.
  - What Minto says about her own limits: the page-cited text ties MECE to structural order, a way of checking a division of a whole (see item 9). She did not claim it for problems with feedback loops. Her Appendix A on "structureless situations" may state a boundary; I have not read it.
- **Verdict.** Roger Martin: confirmed by primary (his own page). Rittel and Webber quotes: confirmed by primary. R&W applied to MECE: your inference, no source says it.

### 13. When answer-first is the wrong order: bad news, hostile readers, exploration

- **Minto herself.** Yes, she addresses it, in more than one place.
  - **Argument before action when the point will surprise the reader.** Page-cited (p. 66): the rule of thumb is action before argument, but the argument can matter more "when the point you are making at the top of the pyramid is alien to the kind of thing he expects you to say." Her example: "Tell me how to cut my costs" answered with "forget cutting costs". Source: https://manager138.com/resources/minto_pyramid_principle/ch5_deduction_and_induction.html, heading "Present action, then argument".
  - **Variants of the introduction.** The book's Ch. 4 has "Why that order?" (the order Situation, Complication, Question). Tam's notes list four introduction orders (standard, direct, "concerned", "aggressive"). Secondary and unverified in the book itself: https://www.adrian.idv.hk/2017-12-20-minto/, Ch. 4 notes.
  - **Alternatives and "the reader knows"** (p. 135, Part 3): alternatives go in the Complication only if the reader already knows them. Page-cited, secondary.
  - 1996 table of contents lists "Difficult Introductory Forms" in Appendix B (p. 221). Text not read.
- **Bad news / hostile readers.** **Not found** in Minto's own text. A Future Talent Learning explainer says the direct approach may not suit "a potentially hostile audience" or exploratory work, but that is the explainer's view, not Minto's (https://www.futuretalentlearning.com/programme-resources/nutshell-bottom-up-top-down-structuring-communications-with-the-pyramid-principle). General business-communication teaching says to use an indirect approach for bad news (Venecia Williams and Jordan Smith, *Fundamentals of Business Communication*, BCcampus, chapter "Negative Messages": https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/businesswritingessentials/chapter/8-3-negative-messages/). That book does not mention Minto.
- **Exploratory work.** She treats the pyramid as the end state of thinking, not a start. The Minto-side framing is "work out thinking first, from the bottom up" (her site and interview). Minto's own passage on exploratory or hypothesis work: not read.
- **Verdict.** Minto addresses surprising or unwelcome points: secondary only (page-cited, p. 66). Bad news specifically: not found for Minto.

### 14. Reduction to slide formatting: "action titles", three bullets

- **Minto's own position.**
  - The method is in Part 1 and 2; Part 4 "Logic in Presentation" is about not losing the structure when it moves to prose or slides (preface). Ch. 11 "Reflecting the Pyramid on a Screen" has "Designing Text Slides" and "Storyboarding".
  - Her heading advice is about ideas, not labels. Page-cited (p. 30, p. 41): state headings "so they reflect ideas rather than categories"; never a heading called "Findings" or "Conclusions"; "Never write about categories, only about ideas." This is the book's source for what consultants now call action titles. The phrase "action title" itself: **not found** in her sources.
