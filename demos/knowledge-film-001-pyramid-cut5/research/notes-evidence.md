## 1. Miller (1956), "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two"

- **Citation**: Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information. *Psychological Review*, 63, 81-97. (Issue number not verified.)
- **Claim**: Across several kinds of test material the span of immediate memory is "about seven items in length". For binary items it is about nine, and about five for monosyllabic English words (his wording: "about nine ... drops to about five with monosyllabic English words"). He separates *bits* from *chunks*: bits are constant for absolute judgment, chunks are constant for immediate memory. Recoding items into chunks is how people stretch the limit; he calls linguistic recoding "the very lifeblood of the thought processes".
- **Self-description**: he opens with "I have been persecuted by an integer" and ends by suspecting the recurring sevens may be "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence".
- **How later work revised it**: see item 2. Cowan (2001) says Miller's number "was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit".
- **Verdict**: confirmed from original text, with one limit: the copy I read is the Classics in the History of Psychology full-text transcription, with no page numbers. Re-open the journal scan for page locators before putting a page number on screen.
- **Source**: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/
- **Locator**: sections "The Span of Immediate Memory" and "Recoding"; the opening paragraph; the closing paragraph.
- **Does not show**: that readers or listeners can take in only seven points of an argument. The tasks were absolute judgment and immediate recall of short lists (digits, words, tones), not comprehension of prose or documents.

## 2. Cowan (2001), "The magical number 4 in short-term memory"

- **Citation**: Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87-114. Cambridge Core lists "published online 30 October 2001" and issue date February 2001.
- **Finding** (abstract): "A single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks". The abstract says others had suggested "three to five chunks", and the target article argues the smaller limit is real.
- **Conditions**: the limit is observable only under four boundary conditions: (1) information overload limits chunks to individual stimulus items; (2) other steps block recoding items into larger chunks; (3) performance discontinuities caused by the limit; (4) indirect effects of the limit. Under these conditions "rehearsal and long-term memory cannot be used to combine stimulus items into chunks".
- **Follow-up** (verified separately): Cowan (2010), *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 19(1), 51-57, doi:10.1177/0963721409359277: "a central memory store limited to 3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults", visible when rehearsal and grouping are prevented. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/
- **Verdict**: confirmed from original (publisher abstract page). I did not open the full article, so I have no page locators inside it.
- **Source**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496
- **Does not show**: that four is a rule for how many items a document should carry. It is a limit measured when chunking is blocked. In ordinary reading, grouping is exactly what is allowed, which is the film's point only by analogy, not by this evidence.

## 3. Bransford & Johnson (1972), "Contextual prerequisites for understanding"

- **Citation**: Bransford, J. D., & Johnson, M. K. (1972). Contextual prerequisites for understanding: Some investigations of comprehension and recall. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 717-726. (Volume, pages and issue confirmed on ERIC EJ068571 and in the scan; received 13 March 1972.) Authors at SUNY Stony Brook. Footnote 1: senior authorship was decided by a coin toss.
- **Verdict**: confirmed from original. I read a scan hosted by Marcia Johnson's Yale lab, which gave the abstract, Experiment I and Table 1, and two other scanned transcriptions (idoc.pub and Scribd excerpts), which gave Experiments II-IV and Tables 2-3. Table 1 agrees across all copies, and Tables 2-3 agree across the two copies that carry them. These are OCR text, so before any digit goes on screen re-open pages 723-724 of the Yale scan.
- **Sources**: https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1972_Bransford_Johnson_JVLVB.pdf (Experiment I, Table 1); https://idoc.pub/documents/bransford-johnson-1972-contextual-prerequisites-for-understanding-1-3no0y93gx5nd (Experiments II-IV, Tables 2-3); https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ068571 (citation).

### Contradicts the common retelling: which experiment is the "washing clothes" one

The common retelling says people saw a washing-clothes picture first. That is wrong. In the paper:

- **Experiment I** used a picture as context, but the passage was about a serenade (balloons, wire, a window, a fellow shouting). It is not the washing-clothes passage. The authors say the underlying topic would be something like "Possible breakdowns in communication during a serenade".
- **Experiment II** is the washing-clothes experiment with Passage A. Context was a one-sentence **topic**, not a picture.
- **Experiment III** repeats it with Passage B, a slightly longer version of A, and also uses "washing clothes".
- **Experiment IV** used a different topic, "making and flying a kite".

### Experiment II (washing clothes, Passage A), exact conditions and numbers

- **Subjects**: 52 students enrolled in a human learning course at SUNY Stony Brook. Groups: No Topic 17, Topic After 17, Topic Before 18. Assigned by randomizing instruction sheets in blocks of three and passing booklets out in a classroom.
- **Delivery**: the experimenter read the passage aloud (single auditory presentation). Subjects then rated comprehension and wrote recall, five minutes for recall, about two minutes after the end of the passage.
- **Topic Before**: the instruction sheet said, "The paragraph you will hear will be about washing clothes."
- **Topic After**: after the passage, the comprehension-rating instructions said, "It may help you to know that the paragraph was about washing clothes."
- **Comprehension scale**: 1 = very difficult to comprehend, 4 = moderate, 7 = very easy. **Recall**: mean number of idea units recalled, maximum possible 18.

| Table 2, Experiment II | No Topic | Topic After | Topic Before | Maximum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean comprehension rating (SE) | 2.29 (.22) | 2.12 (.26) | 4.50 (.49) | 7 |
| Mean idea units recalled (SE) | 2.82 (.60) | 2.65 (.53) | 5.83 (.49) | 18 |

- **Tests**: Topic Before beat No Topic and Topic After on comprehension, Dunnett d(3,49) = 4.46 and 4.80, p < .005; on recall, d(3,49) = 3.97 and 4.20, p < .005.
- **Note**: topic given after the passage did not help; it scored slightly below no topic on both measures (2.12 vs 2.29, 2.65 vs 2.82), though the paper does not test that small difference.

### Experiment III (Passage B, washing clothes)

- Subjects: 21 high school volunteers; Topic After 10, Topic Before 11. Topic given verbally. Comprehension 3.40 (.48) after vs 5.27 (.27) before (max 7); recall 3.30 (.66) vs 7.00 (.43) (max 20). F(1,19) = 12.24 for comprehension and 20.03 for recall, p < .005.

### Experiment IV (kite sentences) and Experiment I, for completeness

- **IV**: 31 high school students (No Topic 9, Topic After 11, Topic Before 11). Comprehension 2.44 / 3.82 / 4.00 (max 7); recall 3.22 / 3.18 / 5.54 (max 14). Topic Before beat No Topic on comprehension, p < .05, but did **not** differ significantly from Topic After on comprehension (p > .05). Recall: Topic Before beat both, p < .05.
- **I** (serenade, picture context): 50 high school students, five groups of 10. Table 1, comprehension (max 7): No context(1) 2.30, No context(2, heard twice) 3.60, Context after 3.30, Partial context 3.70, Context before 6.10. Recall (max 14): 3.60, 3.80, 3.60, 4.00, 8.00. Context Before beat all other four groups, comprehension d(5,45) > 4.19 and recall d(5,45) > 4.12, p < .005. If a retelling says "8 vs 3.6 ideas" or "6.1 comprehension", those are Experiment I (serenade), not washing clothes.
- **Authors' own conclusion**: "knowledge of the topic of a passage may be neither necessary nor sufficient for optimal comprehension". Context needs to be present during comprehension, and a topic after the passage was at "a considerable disadvantage".

- **Does not show**: that conclusion-first writing improves business documents. The passages were deliberately vague paragraphs, heard aloud by students, and the context was the topic or a picture, not a conclusion or recommendation. It shows that a framing given before the content helps comprehension and recall of otherwise opaque material, and that the same framing given after does not. In Experiment IV the before/after difference on comprehension was not significant.

## 4. Ausubel (1960), advance organizers, and later meta-analysis

- **Citation**: Ausubel, D. P. (1960). The use of advance organizers in the learning and retention of meaningful verbal material. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 51, 267-272. (doi 10.1037/h0046669.)
- **One line**: tests the hypothesis that learning and retention of unfamiliar but meaningful verbal material can be facilitated by the advance introduction of relevant subsuming concepts ("organizers"), on the assumption that cognitive structure is hierarchically organized. (From the abstract on Semantic Scholar.)
- **Meta-analysis**: Luiten, J., Ames, W., & Ackerson, G. (1980). A meta-analysis of the effects of advance organizers on learning and retention. *American Educational Research Journal*, 17(2), 211-218. ERIC abstract (read directly): 135 studies examined; "Advance organizers were shown to have a small facilitative effect."
- **Numbers**: the commonly quoted mean effect sizes (0.21 for learning, 0.26 for retention; 110 and 50 effect sizes) came from a search-engine summary, not from a page I could open. I did not see them in the ERIC record.
- **Verdict**: Ausubel citation confirmed (bibliographic records only; I did not read the paper). Luiten et al. "small effect" confirmed from ERIC; the two effect-size numbers are **secondary only**.
- **Sources**: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-use-of-advance-organizers-in-the-learning-and-Ausubel/aae103c3783d102069805e9d6a1c0d61c8c49de4 ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ233231
- **Locator**: Ausubel, abstract; Luiten et al., ERIC abstract. Bransford & Johnson's own reference list cites Ausubel's *Educational Psychology: A Cognitive View* (1968) in their discussion, not the 1960 paper.
- **Does not show**: that an answer or conclusion works as an advance organizer. An organizer is a framing concept introduced before study material, and this was tested on students learning passages. The meta-analysis effect is small.

## 5. BLUF in US Army writing: Army Regulation 25-50

- **Citation**: Army Regulation 25-50, *Information Management: Records Management: Preparing and Managing Correspondence*, Headquarters, Department of the Army, Washington, DC, **10 October 2020**, effective 10 November 2020. Cover note: "This regulation supersedes AR 25-50, dated 17 May 2013."

  - a. "Effective Army writing is understood by the reader in a single rapid reading and is clear, concise, and well-organized in accordance with PL 111-274."

- **Verdict**: confirmed from original wording, but the copy I read is a state-hosted copy (Maine DVEM) of the 10 October 2020 edition, not the Army Publishing Directorate file. Re-open on the Army publishing site before using it as an official citation.

I did not check the 2013 edition, so I cannot say when the phrase first entered the regulation, and I did not verify any "origin of BLUF" claim.
- **Does not show**: that BLUF improves comprehension or decisions. It is a writing requirement, and the standard gives no data.

## 6. The journalistic inverted pyramid

- **Citation**: Pöttker, H. (2003). News and its communicative quality: the inverted pyramid—when and why did it appear? *Journalism Studies*, 4(4), 501-511. doi:10.1080/1461670032000136596.
- **Definition** (abstract): "the inverted pyramid (lead-and-body principle, answers to four or five w-questions at the beginning of the article)".
- **Origin finding** (abstract): the general assumption is that it became a professional standard during the American Civil War (1861-65), for technological (unreliable telegraph), political or commercial reasons. But a content analysis of the *New York Herald* and *New York Times* "shows that the inverted pyramid became commonplace only two decades later". Pöttker's own conclusion is that it grew out of "professional effort to strengthen the communicative quality of news", and he places the shift between about 1875 and 1895, mainly the 1880s. (The 1875-1895 range and the Herald/Times sampling dates, 1855, 1875, 1895, 1920, come from an OCR copy of the full article on Scribd; I did not check the table digits.)
- **Verdict**: confirmed from original (publisher abstract); body details from a scan copy.
- **Source**: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1461670032000136596
- **Contradicts common retellings**: the popular "invented in the Civil War because of the telegraph" story is exactly the assumption Pöttker disputes. The retelling should be avoided or attributed as a disputed account.
- **Does not show**: that inverted-pyramid readers comprehend or decide better. It is a historical content analysis of newspapers, not a reader experiment. It also does not make the inverted pyramid the same thing as Minto's pyramid: the news form orders by importance within a story, not by grouped logic.

## 7. Columbia accident (2003): the Boeing "Debris Assessment" slide, CAIB, Tufte

- **Primary source read**: Columbia Accident Investigation Board, *Report Volume I*, August 2003, https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/caib/news/report/pdf/vol1/full/caib_report_volume1.pdf
- **Verdict**: confirmed from original. I extracted the report text and read the pages quoted below. Page numbers are the printed page numbers (they matched the PDF page numbers in this file). Another edition may paginate differently: one secondary source placed the sidebar on page 192, so check your copy.

### Where it is

- **Chapter 7, "The Accident's Organizational Causes"**, inside section 7.5, in a sidebar titled **"ENGINEERING BY VIEWGRAPHS", page 191** (section 7.5 begins on p. 189, 7.6 on p. 192). The slide is reproduced there with Tufte's annotations ("The analysis by Dr. Edward Tufte of the slide from the Debris Assessment Team briefing").
- Related text: p. 160 (Chapter 6, "Flight Day Nine, Friday, January 24, 2003"), where the briefing is described and Chapter 7 is cross-referenced for "how the poor construction of these viewgraphs effectively minimized key assumptions and uncertainties"; p. 182 (Chapter 7); p. 201 (Chapter 7).

### Slide title and the numbers (as reproduced on p. 191)

- **Title**: "Review Of Test Data Indicates Conservatism for Tile Penetration" (the report prints "Of" capitalized).
- **Lower-level bullet**: under "Flight condition is significantly outside of test database", a sub-bullet reads: "Volume of ramp is 1920cu in vs 3 cu in for test". Earlier on the slide: "Varies with volume/mass of projectile(e.g., 200ft/sec for 3cu. In)".
- **CAIB text on p. 191**: "Only at the bottom of the slide do engineers state a key piece of information: that one estimate of the debris that struck Columbia was 640 times larger than the data used to calibrate the model". It adds in parentheses: "(Later analysis showed that the debris object was actually 400 times larger)." (1920 / 3 = 640, consistent.) p. 160 says the same: Crater "was being used on a piece of debris that was 400 times larger than the standard in Boeing's database. (At the time, the team believed that the debris was 640 times larger.)"
- **Other facts from p. 191**: the slide "created six levels of hierarchy" that "prioritized information that was already contained in 11 simple sentences". Tufte says "The vaguely quantitative words 'significant' and 'significantly' are used 5 times on this slide". "Cubic inches" is written three different ways ("3cu. In," "1920cu in," "3 cu in"). The word "it" in "Test results do show that it is possible at sufficient mass and velocity" actually refers to "damage to the protective tiles". Tufte says the title's "Conservatism" refers not to predicted tile damage but to the choice of test models, and suggests the headline "Review of Test Data Indicates Irrelevance of Two Models".
- **CAIB conclusions on PowerPoint**: p. 191, "The Board views the endemic use of PowerPoint briefing slides instead of technical papers as an illustration of the problematic methods of technical communication at NASA." p. 182: "Unlike NASA, PowerPoint briefings and papers for technical seminars are not substitutes for completed staff work" (said of the Navy's reactor program). p. 201: the Mission Evaluation Room manager "perceived the Boeing analysis as rigorous and quantitative"; "The choice of headings, arrangement of information, and size of bullets on the key chart served to highlight what management already believed."

### Tufte's essay title

- CAIB endnote 39 (p. 194) cites: "Edward R. Tufte, 'The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint,' (Cheshire, CT: Graphics Press, May 2003)". The CAIB also cites Tufte's *Beautiful Evidence* (endnote 38, "in press").
- A second edition, 2006, is titled *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint: Pitching Out Corrupts Within* (Graphics Press); I confirmed that title only from bookseller and Google Books/Library of Congress search listings, not from Tufte's own site (the page I tried returned 404). Use the 2003 title if you cite what the CAIB cited.

### Contradicts common retellings or needs care

1. **1920 cu in is the "ramp", not "the actual debris"**. The slide says "Volume of ramp is 1920cu in". The CAIB then says the debris was later estimated at 400 times the test size, not 640. So "tested at 3 cu in vs actual 1920 cu in" misstates both the object (the ramp, not the debris) and the later revised figure. Do not state 1920 as the actual debris volume.
2. **The slide date**: the report's reproduction shows "2/21/03" in its footer, after the 1 February 2003 loss and after the 24 January briefing. I could not explain this (it could be an auto-date field or a later file date; that is a guess). Do not put a date on this slide on screen.
3. The key figure is not hidden, but placed at the bottom of a hierarchy. The CAIB says it appears "only at the bottom of the slide". It does not say PowerPoint caused the accident; it calls the pattern "an illustration of the problematic methods of technical communication". The same CAIB pages (p. 160, p. 201) put the major weight on organisational and management causes.
4. The CAIB reproduction is the report's own page with Tufte's annotations: the analysis of the language comes from Tufte, quoted by the CAIB, not from CAIB engineers.

- **Does not show**: that a prose memo would have changed the decision. The report supports that the slide format buried a key number and misprioritised it; the counterfactual is not tested.

## 10. Empirical tests of conclusion-first versus conclusion-last

- **Overall verdict**: **not found** in the strong form you want, meaning a direct test of Minto-style answer-first against conclusion-last in business documents, with decision quality, for executives or managers. What exists is a small set of experiments with mixed results, mostly on recall, reading time and reader opinion, and most of it from abstracts I could read but not full texts. None of it supports a blanket claim that answer-first is better.
- **Studies I can cite (abstract-level unless noted)**:
  1. **Li, Q., Karreman, J., & de Jong, M. D. T. (2020).** Inductively versus deductively structured product descriptions: Effects on Chinese and Western readers. *Journal of Business and Technical Communication*, 34(4). doi:10.1177/1050651920932192. Open access. 2 x 3 experiment (text structure x cultural background); readers read a refrigerator product description ending in or opening with a purchase recommendation; measures were recall (eight multiple-choice questions), reading time and opinions. Result: all six conditions recalled "about six out of eight"; no main effect of text structure and no interaction on recall; no effect of text structure on reading time (cultural background had a large effect on reading time, F(2,150) = 26.36, partial eta squared .26, mostly a language effect); Chinese readers rated the inductive text higher on readability and persuasiveness, Western readers rated both equally. Read in the SAGE page text, including the results section. **Verdict: confirmed from original.**
  2. **Spyridakis, J. H., & Fukuoka, W. (2002).** The effect of inductively versus deductively organized text on American and Japanese readers. *IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication*. Abstract only: Americans "performed equally well with either organizational structure"; Japanese readers "recalled more information from inductively organized text". Volume and pages not verified. **Verdict: abstract only.**
  3. **Jansen, F., & Janssen, D. (online January 2011).** Explanations first: A case for presenting explanations before the decision in Dutch bad-news messages. *Journal of Business and Technical Communication*. Two experiments (insurance denial letters, job rejection letters; then e-mail): readers perceived the indirect order (explanation first) as "more comprehensible and agreeable", the writer as more competent and empathic, and were more inclined to comply. Volume and pages not verified. **Verdict: abstract only.** This is evidence for conclusion-last in bad-news messages.
  4. **Kieras, D. E. (1980).** Initial mention as a signal to thematic content in technical passages. *Memory & Cognition*. Technical passages from *Scientific American*: when the theme sentence came first, readers' statements of the main idea resembled it more than when it was embedded in the middle. Reading times were "58.3 sec for the theme-first condition and 60.1 sec for the theme-embedded condition", a difference that did not approach significance. Volume and pages not verified; read through an excerpt of the article text. **Verdict: secondary excerpt of the original.** Supports that readers treat first-mentioned content as the main idea; found no reading-time saving.
  5. **Devillier, J. L. (1972).** Communication effects of variations in organization and format. *ABCA Journal of Business Communication*, 9(3). Abstract: "Neither logical nor psychological order has any statistically significant superiority." Full text not accessed (paywalled); I do not know what was manipulated or the sample. **Verdict: abstract only; do not rely on.**
- **Sources**: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1050651920932192 ; https://exa.ai/library/publication/jzfwj005w67 (Spyridakis & Fukuoka listing); https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/1050651910380372 ; https://exa.ai/library/publication/9sr3h3774q6 (Kieras excerpt); https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002194367200900301
- **Does not show**: that answer-first improves comprehension, reading speed or decision quality. These experiments mostly find no recall or reading-time difference between orders for English-language readers in the cases tested, and one finds a better response to explanation-first in bad news. The one clean effect is in Kieras: readers take the first-placed statement as the main idea. That supports "the first thing you say is taken as the point", not "answer-first is more efficient".

## Safe to state in the film

1. Miller (1956) reported that immediate memory span for many kinds of material was about seven items, and described the number as possibly a "Pythagorean coincidence". He distinguished bits from chunks. (Source: item 1.)
2. Cowan (2001) argued the limit when rehearsal and chunking are blocked is about four chunks, and said Miller's seven was a "rough estimate and a rhetorical device". (Item 2.)
3. Bransford & Johnson (1972), Experiment II: students heard a deliberately vague paragraph. Those told "washing clothes" beforehand rated comprehension 4.50 on a 7-point scale and recalled 5.83 of 18 idea units; with no topic, 2.29 and 2.82; told after the passage, 2.12 and 2.65. N = 52 (17/17/18). Say "told the topic in one sentence", not "shown a picture". (Item 3.)
4. The picture experiment is a different passage (a serenade): Experiment I, comprehension 6.10 vs 2.30, recall 8.00 vs 3.60 of 14, 10 students per group. (Item 3.)
5. AR 25-50 (10 October 2020), paragraph 1-38, names "putting the main point at the beginning of the correspondence (bottom line up front)" as one of two essential requirements of Army writing. (Item 5.)
6. Pöttker (2003) found from New York Herald and Times samples that the inverted pyramid became commonplace around the 1880s, not during the Civil War. (Item 6.)
7. The CAIB report (Vol. I, August 2003, Chapter 7, p. 191, "Engineering by Viewgraphs") reproduced a Boeing slide titled "Review Of Test Data Indicates Conservatism for Tile Penetration", whose bottom bullet reads "Volume of ramp is 1920cu in vs 3 cu in for test". ## Do not state

- That the Bransford & Johnson washing-clothes experiment used a picture, or that its recall maximum was 14 or its numbers were 8 vs 3.6 (that is the serenade Experiment I).
- That Miller's seven or Cowan's four is a limit on how many points a document or slide may carry, or that Minto's grouping is "proven" by either.
- That the actual debris was 1920 cubic inches. The slide says "Volume of ramp". The CAIB later puts the debris at 400 times the test size. Do not give the slide a date; its footer reads "2/21/03" in the report, which I could not explain.
- That PowerPoint caused the Columbia accident, or that the CAIB said so.
- That BLUF is an acronym in AR 25-50 (it is not in the 2020 text), or any claim about where BLUF originated, or that it was shown to improve decisions.
- That the inverted pyramid was invented during the Civil War because of the telegraph (Pöttker disputes it), or that it equals Minto's pyramid.
- The Bezos 2004 email as a verbatim, Amazon-published document, or as "banning" PowerPoint in its own words. It is a 2015 press report of a follow-up email that says "4 page memo".
- Luiten et al.'s effect sizes (0.21 and 0.26) until verified; only "small facilitative effect" is confirmed.
- The McKinsey figure as "reading and answering email" or as a measured number. Do not confuse it with the executive-summary "28 hours".
- Any Porter and Nohria percentage (72%, 61%, 62.5 hours) as read from HBR; they come from excerpts, not the article.
- That experiments show answer-first beats conclusion-last. The studies I found show no recall or reading-time difference for English readers, a first-position cue for main-idea identification (Kieras), and a preference for explanation-first in bad news (Jansen & Janssen).
